Boat pre-departure checklist UK:
before you leave the marina

Before casting off, review the weather and tides, check your route and alternatives, prepare the boat, and brief the crew. Use the checklist below as a starting point, then add the checks required by your own vessel and equipment.

By Helmlore · Updated 19 October 2026 · UK coastal leisure boating

A familiar berth can make a departure feel routine. A repeatable check gives you time to notice a fault while you still have options. Tick a box only after checking; an unchecked item needs a decision, not a hurried assumption.

1. Check the conditions for departure and return

Read an appropriate marine forecast for your area, including wind, visibility and sea state. Look at how conditions may change before you get home, rather than relying on the view from a sheltered berth.

Use current tidal predictions and local harbour information. Check the time basis, entrance restrictions and any depth or stream constraints. Decide what would make you delay or turn back before the trip becomes a commitment. RYA passage-planning guidance provides further context.

2. Plan the whole passage, with an alternative

Use our coastal passage-planning guide and printable template to organise the route, timings and decision points.

Include leaving the berth, the harbour exit, the open-water legs and arrival. Identify hazards and a usable alternative if the intended destination becomes unsuitable. Match the journey to the boat, available daylight and crew capability.

Tell a reliable contact ashore where you intend to go and when you expect to return. Agree what they should do if you are overdue, and remember to close the loop when safely back. A plan is useful only if someone can act on it.

3. Check the boat using its own procedures

Our marine diesel daily-checks guide and printable checklist explains the engine checks before starting and after start-up.

Follow your vessel and engine manuals. Inspect for leaks or changes, check the bilge and verify fuel, fluid levels, steering and essential electrical supplies. Check relevant cooling-water intakes and their required valve positions before starting. Never assume every seacock should be open or closed.

After starting, follow the engine’s checks for cooling-water discharge where applicable, instruments and alarms. Keep clear of moving and hot parts. Check gear operation only where it can be done safely, with the boat controlled and the propeller area clear. Use and test the kill cord where fitted, following the manufacturer’s procedure.

Real-world pitfall: an engine starting successfully does not prove that it has cooling water or enough usable fuel for the passage. Investigate an abnormal indication before departure.

4. Make safety equipment accessible

Wear an appropriate, correctly fitted lifejacket or buoyancy aid for the activity. Check it using the manufacturer’s instructions. Confirm that emergency equipment is suitable, maintained and accessible, rather than buried beneath bags.

Have a working means of calling for help, know your position and ensure the crew can use the equipment. Do not rely on mobile-phone coverage offshore. Prepare an appropriate anchor and rode for the boat and area. The RNLI’s equipment guidance explains these essentials.

5. Give a short, practical crew briefing

Explain the destination, expected conditions and each person’s job. Demonstrate safe movement, lifejacket use and where emergency equipment is kept. Discuss man-overboard actions and how to raise the alarm.

Identify someone who can take over basic control and call for help if the skipper cannot. Invite questions about confidence, mobility, seasickness or other needs that could affect the trip. A briefing works when the crew understand it, not when the skipper has finished speaking.

6. Pause before casting off

Explain the departure manoeuvre: which line comes off when, who handles it, and what happens if you need to stop. Prepare fenders and lines, check shore connections and secure loose equipment.

Look around once more. Check people, nearby craft and the intended escape route. If something no longer fits the plan, pause. There is no benefit in completing a checklist quickly if a new problem has appeared.

Keep checking after you leave

Departure is the start of monitoring, not the end. Maintain a lookout, review the conditions and watch relevant instruments. Record useful observations and reassess the plan when the situation changes.
