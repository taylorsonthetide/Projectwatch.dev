Buying your first boat in the UK: where do I start?

Start with the boating you want to do and the full cost of ownership. Then check the seller and paperwork, agree a written purchase contract with inspection conditions, and commission your own survey and machinery checks before completing. A shiny boat and a successful engine start are only the beginning.

UK recreational boat buying guide · Checked 11 October 2026 · Focus: second-hand boats. New builds and imports have additional risks. This is general education; get advice on your actual contract, title and tax position from a suitably experienced professional.

1. What boat do you actually need?

Write down where you will keep it, where you want to travel, who comes aboard and how often you can use it. A river cruiser, coastal motorboat and offshore sailing yacht solve different problems. Think about draft, air draft, marina access, launch arrangements and whether you can handle the boat with your usual crew.

Before shopping, try boating through a club, practical course or supervised hire. Check whether everyone can move around safely, board from the pontoon and reach the controls. Do not buy extra size or complexity just because it looks like better value.

A berth advertised with a boat may not transfer: confirm directly with the mooring provider before paying a premium. [1]

2. Broker, dealer or private seller?

A competent professional broker can coordinate the transaction and paperwork. Verify the business, current trade-association membership where claimed, and how client money is held. A broker normally acts for the seller; your independent surveyor and any adviser should work for you. Association membership is useful, but does not replace your checks. [2]

The identity of the seller affects your rights
RouteWhat to understand
Trader selling to a consumerThe Consumer Rights Act 2015 generally requires satisfactory quality, fitness for purpose and conformity with description. Age, price and disclosed defects matter when judging a used boat. A warranty is additional to statutory rights. [3]
Genuine private saleYou do not get the same trader-to-consumer quality protection. Description and contractual promises still matter; a private sale is not permission to misdescribe the boat. Keep the advert and written answers. [4]
Brokerage saleA business advertising a boat does not automatically mean the boat is being sold by that business. Check who is named as seller and whether the broker is only their agent. [5]

Do not assume a deposit is refundable because you change your mind, or that every purchase has a cooling-off period. Contract terms and the way the transaction was made matter. If the seller's status is unclear, get advice before signing.

At the first viewing

Ask why it is being sold, how long it has been owned, where it has been used and whether it has been grounded, sunk, storm-damaged, repaired, chartered or heavily modified. Ask about known defects. Get significant answers in writing. Inspect lockers and accessible bilges with permission; look for leaks, corrosion, unusual smells and poor repairs. Treat these as questions for an expert, not your own structural verdict.

3. Check ownership before committing money

Match the boat's identity to the paperwork: name, hull/craft identification number where fitted, registration number and engine details. Ask for the seller's identity, the chain of Bills of Sale, any builder's certificate, registration records and authority to sell. Company-owned boats, joint owners and estates need appropriate authority and signatures. Missing documents are a problem to resolve, not a reason to rush.

Check outstanding finance, mortgages, charges and claims. Where relevant, obtain a current Part 1 registry transcript and have it checked. Not every liability is visible on a registry. Arrange documented lender settlement and discharge as part of completion; a promise to pay later is inadequate. The Small Ships Register (Part 3/SSR) is not legal proof of ownership. [6]

VAT and import history

Ask for evidence supporting the boat's UK VAT/customs status, such as the original VAT invoice or import documentation. A registration certificate does not prove VAT status. A private UK resale normally does not itself attract VAT, but that does not establish the boat's previous tax or import history. [7] [5]

For a boat abroad or with a cross-border history, establish where it has been, when and under whose ownership. Do not treat an old “VAT paid” statement as a guarantee of present UK status. Import VAT, relief eligibility and Great Britain/Northern Ireland arrangements can differ. Resolve the actual facts with HMRC or a marine tax adviser before buying or moving it. [8]

Construction and compliance documents

Where applicable, ask for the declaration of conformity, identification/builder's plate and owner's manual. Recreational Craft Regulations apply differently in Great Britain and Northern Ireland; scope and exemptions matter. Imported or substantially modified boats may need further assessment. A CE/UKCA mark is not a pre-purchase condition survey, and not every older boat needs a newly applied mark. Check the individual vessel with a competent compliance specialist. [9]

4. Agree the offer, contract and deposit

Submit your proposed price and conditions through the broker, who takes them to the seller. Once accepted, make sure the agreed terms appear in the written sale-and-purchase agreement. Have the agreement checked before signing; do not wait until the survey exposes a problem. [6]

Put these questions to the broker or adviser

  • Does the agreement allow the out-of-water survey, machinery inspection, oil testing and sea trial you need?
  • What findings permit rejection, renegotiation or repair, and how must you give notice?
  • What are the deadlines for reports, acceptance and completion? Will laboratory turnaround fit?
  • How are extensions agreed if weather or yard availability delays the trial or lift?
  • Who pays inspection, skipper, lift, storage and relaunch costs, including if you reject the boat?
  • Who bears risk during inspections and when does risk pass to you?
  • What equipment is included? Attach a precise inventory.

A deposit of around 10% is common, not a legal requirement. Under the usual brokerage arrangement it is held as stakeholder in a designated client account. Understand the exact holding and release terms. Your right to its return depends on the contract and compliance with its notice procedure. [2]

Before making a bank transfer

Confirm the payee and client-account details using an independently verified telephone number. Treat emailed changes of bank details as a reason to stop and verify. Keep the signed agreement and payment receipt. A client account is a safeguard, not a blanket guarantee against fraud or loss.

5. Commission your own survey and lift-out

For a used boat, Helmlore recommends an independent pre-purchase condition survey before completion. A seller's old survey, valuation or insurance inspection may have a different scope and client. A Boat Safety Scheme examination is not a substitute for a full purchase survey. [1]

Choose a surveyor experienced in the boat type and construction material. Check qualifications, relevant experience, professional indemnity cover, conflicts of interest and written terms. Agree the scope, access, exclusions, reporting date and whether the insurer accepts the resulting report. A valuation may be an extra service. [10]

Why take it out of the water?

An afloat viewing cannot reveal all underwater surfaces and fittings. Arrange lift-out or another suitable inspection method with the surveyor and yard. Allow time for cleaning and safe access as advised. Confirm lifting arrangements, insured responsibilities and whether relaunch is included.

Ask the surveyor to explain which of these areas are included, and which need specialist work:

  • Hull, deck and structural areas; previous damage and repairs; moisture or corrosion concerns.
  • Underwater fittings, through-hulls/seacocks, keel attachments where fitted, rudder, propeller and drive components.
  • Steering, accessible tanks and hoses, bilge systems, electrics, batteries and shore power.
  • Safety equipment, fire protection and gas installation; a qualified gas/electrical specialist where needed.
  • For sailing boats, rig, chainplates and sails; whether an aloft or separate rig inspection is required.
  • For steel or aluminium craft, whether thickness measurement or corrosion investigation is appropriate.

These are scoping questions, not a promise that every survey tests everything. Locked spaces, lining, weather and lack of dismantling can limit inspection. Get unanswered questions investigated before accepting the boat. A survey is an assessment at that time, not a warranty against every hidden defect. [11]

6. Engines, servicing and oil analysis

Do not assume “full hull survey” includes a full engine survey. Helmlore recommends a separate machinery inspection by an engineer familiar with that engine and drive, especially where propulsion represents a large part of the boat's value. Agree the tests with them; invasive testing needs permission and is not appropriate for every engine. [12]

What to ask the engineer to assess

  • Service invoices and actual work done, engine identity, recorded hours and periods laid up.
  • Cold starting where practicable, leaks, smoke, cooling, exhaust, mounts and accessible fuel systems.
  • Operation under load, instruments, charging, gearbox and propulsion drive.
  • Manufacturer diagnostics, cylinder/compression checks or further investigation where appropriate.
  • Generator and auxiliary engines separately; their condition is not established by checking the main engine.

A low hour reading does not establish good condition. Ask about calendar-based maintenance as well as hours: cooling-system work, hoses, belts, impellers, drive servicing and manufacturer-specific requirements. Request a written report and quotations for recommended work. [12]

Send oil to a laboratory before the purchase decision

Arrange sampling with the engineer and a suitable laboratory. Depending on the machinery, consider engine oil, gearbox oil and coolant. Fluid analysis can reveal indicators of wear, contamination, fuel dilution and coolant problems. It supports the inspection; it cannot certify the engine as fault-free. [13]

Fresh oil can make the picture less useful

Record the oil type, engine hours, and hours since the last oil change. Recently changed oil has had little time to accumulate wear material; an unknown oil history limits interpretation. Ask the engineer how this affects the result rather than treating a clean sample as proof. Previous samples and trends are more informative than a single result. Wait for the laboratory report and explanation before the relevant contract deadline. [11] [13]

7. Sea trial, survey findings and your decision

Arrange a suitably insured and competently skippered trial under the agreed conditions. It tests operation afloat and suitability for you; it does not replace the lift-out or engineering inspection. Agree the duration, who attends and the systems to demonstrate. Weather may require an agreed extension rather than a rushed trip. [2]

Ask the engineer/surveyor to observe the relevant machinery under safe operating loads. Have steering, controls, manoeuvring and agreed onboard equipment demonstrated. For a sailing boat, include a sail where practical. Record what could not be tested and why. Do not operate an unfamiliar boat alone to “see what happens”.

Read the report before deciding

Discuss each serious finding and every exclusion with your surveyor. Separate immediate safety work, expensive repairs, routine servicing and cosmetic items. Obtain independent repair quotes and assess the whole first-season cost.

Accept

When condition, documents and costs meet your needs, give acceptance as required by the agreement.

Resolve

Agree a revised price or precisely specified repairs in writing, with evidence and reinspection where needed.

Reject

If the contract allows rejection, follow its grounds, deadline and notice method. Get advice promptly if disputed.

A survey finding does not automatically oblige the seller to reduce the price. Nor does rejecting a boat necessarily refund your survey, yard or engineer fees. Keep all correspondence and do not let a notice deadline expire while casually negotiating. [6]

8. Completion, insurance and handover

Before releasing the balance, confirm the completion arrangements, final inventory, original documents and any agreed lender discharge or repairs. Check the boat again if time has passed since inspection. Put insurance in place from the point your agreement transfers risk, with any delivery voyage covered. Confirm survey recommendations or conditions with your insurer. [14]

Receive the properly executed Bill of Sale, completion statement, relevant title/VAT/compliance documents, manuals, service history, keys and equipment. Complete the applicable registry transfer or new registration process; do not just keep the previous owner's certificate. [5]

Ask for a practical handover

Have someone show you shore-power isolation, batteries, fuel shut-offs, seacocks, bilge pumps, fire systems, emergency steering, engine checks and installed navigation/radio equipment. Record explanations with permission. Arrange overdue maintenance and safety work before use, then build confidence with short local trips and practical tuition.

Check the rules for where you intend to keep and operate it. Inland navigation-authority registration/licensing, insurance and BSS requirements can apply; sea registration and radio requirements are separate. A purchase contract does not grant permission to use every waterway. [15]

New boats, projects and imported boats

New boat: agree specification, delivery, inspection/acceptance, warranty and snagging procedures. For staged payments, get advice on title to the boat/work in progress, security for payments and what happens if the builder fails. Consider an independent surveyor for construction and handover. [14]

Project boat: price the yard time, access, waste disposal, transport and unavailable parts as well as repairs. A cheap purchase can still be an expensive ownership commitment. Decide whether you can complete the work safely and legally.

Import: resolve customs, tax, compliance, registration and transport before committing. Do not assume an overseas bargain remains cheaper after these costs. Great Britain, Northern Ireland and EU rules are not interchangeable. [8] [9]

Reasons to pause the purchase

  • Seller identity, boat identity or ownership documents do not match.
  • Pressure to pay quickly, unexpected bank changes or refusal of independent checks.
  • Unclear deposit/refund terms, missing inventory or an unrealistic inspection deadline.
  • Unresolved finance, VAT/import history or compliance questions.
  • Missing service records, unexplained repairs or tests that could not be completed.
  • Repairs exceed your reserve, or insurance/berthing cannot be secured on workable terms.

Pause and obtain evidence or advice. Walking away from an unsuitable boat can be a successful outcome.
